# Patterns

Each entry gives the **tell** — what it sounds like in a career conversation — and the move it routes to. The moves are defined at the bottom.

Match on the tell, not on the label. The user never says "I am anchoring."

## Errors in the judgement

**Inside view** — _"Six months and I'll be ready to switch."_
Priced from the details of this plan, as if it were the only one that ever ran. Every plan is late by the same optimism.
→ Outside view.

**WYSIATI** — a confident story built from exactly the facts that happen to be in the room.
The story hangs together because nothing missing is in it. Coherence is not evidence.
→ Ask three people. Prototype conversation.

**Availability** — _"Everyone I know who tried that burned out."_
Three vivid cases standing in for a rate. The memorable ones are memorable because they were unusual.
→ Outside view.

**Base-rate neglect** — _"This role is perfect for me, so I'll get it."_
Fit judged by resemblance, with the actual odds of the pool never looked at.
→ Outside view.

**Overconfidence** — a single future, described in detail, with no range around it.
Detail feels like knowledge and is not. The confident version is the one that skipped the failure branches.
→ Pre-mortem.

**Hindsight** — _"Taking that job was obviously a mistake."_
Judging a past decision by how it turned out, which makes the world look more predictable than it was, and makes the next decision feel safer than it is.
→ Judge the decision by what was knowable then, not by the outcome.

**Loss aversion** — _"I'd be giving up my seniority."_
What is already held counts roughly double what is on offer. This is why people stay in jobs they describe accurately and negatively.
→ Reframe as a stranger's choice: if you held neither position today, which would you take?

**Framing** — the same situation flipping with the wording. _"A 20% pay cut"_ and _"80% of my salary to do work I want"_ are one fact and two decisions.
→ State it both ways and see whether the answer holds.

**Sunk cost** — _"After five years in this field, I can't just leave."_
Years already spent are gone under either choice, so they belong in neither column.
→ Reframe as a stranger's choice.

**Halo** — one attribute of a company, a person, or a title spreading into a whole judgement. A famous employer becomes a good manager, good pay, and good work.
→ Prototype conversation, aimed at the specific attribute that matters.

**Remembering self over experiencing self** — _"It'll look great on my CV."_
Choosing the memory of the job over the Tuesdays inside it. Memory keeps the peaks and the ending and drops the duration; the duration is what you actually live.
→ Good-time journal. Ask what an ordinary Tuesday in that job costs and gives.

## Errors in the question

**Gravity problem** — _"I want to change fields but I'm not starting over at 35."_
Stated as a problem, but no action moves it: it is a condition. Energy spent arguing with it is energy not spent designing inside it.
The test: name an action that would change it. If none exists, it is gravity.
→ Accept it, and reopen the question with it fixed in place.

**One right path** — _"I need to figure out what I'm meant to do."_
A single correct life, hidden somewhere, waiting to be found. It makes every choice feel final and every uncertainty feel like failure.
→ Three odyssey plans.

**Incoherence** — the stated values and the pursued option pointing in different directions, with the gap unnoticed. Wanting time with family and optimising for the role that eats evenings.
→ Coherence check.

**Analysis where a prototype was cheaper** — a month of comparing two paths on paper, with no one holding either path yet spoken to.
Thinking cannot produce the missing information. Only contact with the real thing can.
→ Prototype conversation, then prototype experience.

**Never letting go** — a decision made, then reopened weekly against the option not taken.
Re-litigating a closed choice lowers satisfaction without improving the choice.
→ Choose, then close it. Set the date you will next review it, and leave it shut until then.

**Deciding by continued analysis** — waiting for the spreadsheet to produce a feeling of certainty it cannot produce.
Analysis narrows the options; it does not pick. The last step is a judgement call.
→ Narrow to two or three, then decide and close it.

## Errors about yourself

**Interest mistaken for skill** — _"I love writing, so I should write for a living."_
Loving a thing and being good at it are separate facts. Either can exist without the other.
→ Ask three people. Good-time journal.

**Unchecked self-model** — a whole plan built on a self-description no one else has ever confirmed.
What you are good at is partly observable from outside and partly not from inside.
→ Ask three people.

**Values read as skills** — _"I'm a people person, so I should manage."_
What you value in an environment and what you are able to do in it are two axes. Conflating them puts people in roles that match their taste and not their ability.
→ Separate the two lists explicitly, then check the option against each.

**Unexamined money story** — a number treated as a floor or a ceiling with no account of where it came from. Often inherited, and often no longer true.
→ Say the number out loud, then say what it is for. Check whether the reason survives being spoken.

## Moves

**Outside view** — name the reference class (people who made this switch, candidates in this pool, projects like this one), find what actually happened to them, and use that record as the starting number. Adjust for your case afterwards, not before.

**Pre-mortem** — it is two years on and the plan failed. Write the story of how. Five minutes, specific causes. It licenses the doubts nobody voices while a plan is being agreed to.

**Prototype conversation** — find someone already living the option and ask for their story, not their advice. What their week looks like, what surprised them, what they gave up. Cheap, and it carries the information no amount of thinking generates.

**Prototype experience** — a small, bounded, low-cost trial of the option: a side project, a shadow day, a short contract, a volunteer stint. The point is not to prove you are right. It is to be wrong cheaply and early.

**Three odyssey plans** — sketch three five-year lives: the current path continued; the same life if that path vanished tomorrow; the life you would build if money and other people's opinions were not factors. Each gets a title and the question it would answer. The point is felt, not written: more than one good life is available.

**Good-time journal** — for two weeks, log what you did, how engaged you were, and whether it left you with more energy or less. Engagement and energy are different axes. Behaviour recorded beats self-image recalled.

**Ask three people** — ask three people who have seen you work: what do you come to me for, and what am I visibly good at. Ask for examples, not adjectives.

**Coherence check** — write two paragraphs: why you work, and what makes a life good. Then hold the option against both. Where they disagree, the disagreement is the finding.

**Reframe as a stranger's choice** — if you were handed this situation today, holding none of it, what would you pick? Strips the endowment and the sunk cost out in one question.

---

These are frameworks for thinking, drawn from Kahneman and Tversky, Burnett and Evans, and Stahl. They are not validated instruments; Stahl's material in particular is coaching heuristic rather than psychometrics. Use them as prompts for a better question, not as a diagnosis.
